# Men's Hair Types Explained: Straight, Wavy, Curly and Coily

Two men can ask for the same haircut, sit in the same chair, and walk out looking nothing alike. That's not the barber being inconsistent. It's hair type — and it's the single most useful thing to understand about your own head before you choose a style.

Most classification systems sort hair into four families by curl pattern, then subdivide each by how tight that pattern is. You don't need to memorize the chart. You need to know which family you're in and what follows from it.

## Type 1: Straight

Straight hair has no curl pattern. It falls in whatever direction gravity and your growth pattern send it, and it reflects light more directly than other types, which is why it tends to look shinier — and greasier, faster.

**What it does well:** clean lines, sharp fades, precise partings. Straight hair shows the geometry of a cut better than any other type, which is why classic side parts, crops, and razor-sharp skin fades read so well on it.

**Where it fights you:** volume. Straight hair lies flat, and fine straight hair lies flatter still. It also shows every mistake — a fade that isn't perfectly blended on straight hair is immediately visible.

**Practical advice:** get texture cut into the ends rather than relying on product to create movement. Lightweight products only; anything heavy will drag it down within an hour. If your hair goes limp by mid-afternoon, the problem is usually product weight, not product quantity.

## Type 2: Wavy

Wavy hair forms an S-shape rather than a spiral. It ranges from a loose bend that only shows when the hair gets long (2A) to a defined, almost curly wave (2C).

**What it does well:** natural volume and movement with very little effort. Textured crops, medium-length messy styles, and anything that's meant to look casually undone work with wavy hair rather than against it.

**Where it fights you:** unpredictability and frizz. Wavy hair is the type most affected by humidity, and in Mississauga's summer that's a real consideration. It also tends to wave in different directions on different parts of the head, which can make the sides puff out as they grow.

**Practical advice:** a sea salt spray or a light matte cream enhances the wave instead of flattening it. Ask your barber to remove weight from the sides — waves that puff sideways are usually a bulk problem, not a length problem. Avoid brushing dry; it separates the wave pattern into frizz.

## Type 3: Curly

Curly hair forms defined spirals, from loose corkscrews (3A) to tight, springy curls (3B and 3C). This is where the gap between wet length and dry length becomes significant — curly hair can shrink by 30% or more as it dries.

**What it does well:** volume and character. A curly top over a fade is one of the most striking men's looks available, and curly hair holds shape in a way straight hair never will.

**Where it fights you:** dryness and shrinkage. The natural oils from your scalp travel down a curl less easily than a straight shaft, so curly hair is drier by default. And a barber who cuts your hair wet without accounting for shrinkage will hand you something dramatically shorter than you asked for.

**Practical advice:** ask your barber to cut curly hair dry, or at least to check the length dry before finishing. Use a leave-in conditioner or curl cream rather than a drying gel. Never brush dry curls — use fingers or a wide-tooth comb while damp.

## Type 4: Coily

Coily hair forms very tight coils or zigzags, sometimes so tight the curl pattern isn't visible to the eye. It has the most volume per strand of any type and the highest shrinkage — some coily hair appears a quarter of its actual length.

**What it does well:** sculptural shapes. Coily hair holds a defined outline better than any other type, which is why sponge twists, high-top shapes, and precisely edged cuts look so clean on it.

**Where it fights you:** moisture retention and breakage. Coily hair is the most fragile type — the tight bends in each strand are structural weak points — and it dries out fastest.

**Practical advice:** moisture is not optional. A daily leave-in or oil is standard maintenance, not a luxury. For cuts, the line-up matters as much as the length, and edges want a barber who works with coily hair regularly rather than one who's improvising.

## How to Identify Your Type in Two Minutes

Wash your hair, don't apply anything, and let it air-dry without touching it. Then look:

- Falls flat and straight → **Type 1**
- Forms a loose S-bend, especially near the ends → **Type 2**
- Forms visible spirals you could wrap around a finger → **Type 3**
- Forms tight coils or a zigzag pattern, with heavy shrinkage → **Type 4**

Most people have more than one pattern on their head. It's common to have looser waves at the front and tighter curls at the crown. Cut for the dominant pattern, and tell your barber where the exceptions are.

## Density and Thickness Are Separate Things

Two more variables affect what your hair will do, and they're often confused:

**Density** is how many hairs you have per square inch. High density means a full head that can support heavy, blunt shapes. Low density means the scalp shows through more easily, and shorter, sharper cuts usually read better than long ones.

**Thickness** (or coarseness) is the diameter of an individual strand. Coarse hair is stubborn — it holds shape but resists styling. Fine hair is easy to style and impossible to keep styled.

You can have fine hair in high density (lots of thin strands, looks full but goes flat) or coarse hair in low density (few thick strands, feels substantial but shows scalp). The combination changes what products and cuts will work.

## What This Means for How Often You Book

Curl pattern affects how gracefully a cut grows out. Straight hair shows the loss of a sharp line quickly — a skin fade on straight hair looks noticeably grown out at two weeks. Curly and coily hair hides regrowth better in the body of the cut, but the edges and line-up go first, usually around the two-week mark.

As a rough guide: skin fades on any hair type want 2–3 weeks; tapers and scissor cuts stretch to 4–6; and if you're growing something out, a shape-up every 6 weeks stops the grow-out phase from looking accidental.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can my hair type change?**
Yes. Hormonal shifts, age, and medication can all alter curl pattern and density. It's common for hair to become finer with age and for curl pattern to loosen or tighten in your twenties and thirties.

**Does hair type determine which cuts I can get?**
It determines which cuts will hold without a fight. You can get almost any cut on almost any hair type — the question is how much daily effort it will take to maintain, and whether that's a trade you want to make.

**Do I need different products for different types?**
Broadly, yes. Straight and fine hair wants lightweight matte products. Wavy hair wants light creams and salt sprays. Curly and coily hair wants moisture — creams, leave-ins, and oils — rather than the drying, high-hold products that suit straight hair.
