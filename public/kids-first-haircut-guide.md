# Your Child's First Haircut: A Barber's Guide to Getting Through It

A first haircut is a small event with an outsized amount of parental anxiety attached to it. The good news is that most of the things that make it go badly are avoidable, and most of them are decided before anyone sits in the chair.

Here's what actually matters, from barbers who cut children's hair every day of the week.

## When to Book the First One

There's no correct age. Most children have their first cut somewhere between nine months and two years, usually driven by hair getting into eyes rather than by a milestone.

What matters more than age is timing within the day. Book around a nap and a meal — a tired or hungry toddler will not sit still, and no amount of preparation compensates for either. **Mid-morning, after breakfast and well before the afternoon nap**, is the single most reliable window.

Weekday mornings are also quieter in most shops, which means less noise, less waiting, and a barber who isn't rushing. If your child is nervous or sensitive to noise, that difference is worth building the appointment around.

## Preparing at Home

**Talk about it in advance, but not too far in advance.** A day or two is plenty. Longer than that and you've created a week of anticipation about something a two-year-old can't picture.

**Use neutral language.** "Cut" and "sharp" and "don't worry" all introduce concepts a child hadn't considered. "The barber is going to make your hair neat" is enough.

**Show them what it looks like.** Bring them along to your own appointment first if you can. Watching a parent sit in the chair, get a haircut, and be perfectly fine is more persuasive than any explanation.

**Practise at home.** Sit them on a chair, put a towel around their shoulders, and run a comb through their hair. The unfamiliarity of the cape is a surprisingly common trigger.

**Don't oversell it as a treat.** If the buildup is enormous, the reality is a letdown. Matter-of-fact works better.

## What to Bring

- **A familiar comfort object** — a toy, a blanket, whatever they hold onto.
- **A tablet or phone with something they like.** Screens are not a parenting failure in a barber's chair; they're the most effective stillness tool available, and every barber will tell you the same.
- **A photo of the cut you want.** Toddler hair is unpredictable and descriptions get misread.
- **A snack for afterward**, not during. Chewing moves the head.

## In the Chair

**Sit them on your lap if they're small or nervous.** Almost every barbershop will let you do this, and for children under about three it's often the easiest arrangement. The child is anchored, they can see you, and the barber has a stable head to work with.

**Stay calm and relaxed yourself.** Children read parental tension instantly. If you're braced for a disaster, they'll be braced too. If you're chatting normally, most kids settle within a minute.

**Let the barber lead.** Barbers who cut children's hair regularly have a routine — showing the clippers, letting the child feel the vibration on their hand first, working quickly around the noisy parts. Interrupting that routine to reassure the child usually restarts the anxiety.

**Don't apologize for your kid.** Every barber who takes children's appointments expects some wriggling, some crying, and the occasional total refusal. It's genuinely routine and nobody's annoyed.

## If They Cry

Some children cry through the entire first haircut. This is common and it's not a sign anything is wrong. Barbers work faster and more carefully when a child is upset, and a slightly less perfect cut done in six minutes is a much better outcome than a perfect cut fought over for twenty.

If a child becomes genuinely distressed — not fussing, but panicked — the right move is to stop. A barber will usually offer this before you have to ask. A half-finished cut can be tidied up another day, and forcing it through creates a child who is frightened of haircuts for years. Almost every barber has seen a child come back three weeks later and sit perfectly still.

## Clippers or Scissors?

For a first haircut, most barbers use scissors. Clippers vibrate and make a sustained noise close to the ear, and that's the most common thing that upsets small children.

Scissors are quieter, but they require the child to hold still longer. A barber will read the child and choose. If your child is noise-sensitive — and you'll know — mention it when you book so the barber can plan around it.

If clippers are needed for the cut you want, a good barber will introduce them gradually: turn them on across the room first, then let the child hold them, then start at the back of the head where they can't see.

## What Cut to Ask For

Keep the first one simple. A trim, a tidy of the fringe, and a clean-up around the ears and neck is plenty. Toddler hair is fine and grows unpredictably, and elaborate shapes rarely survive contact with a pillow.

Practical guidance:

- **Leave a little more length than you think.** Toddler hair shrinks up as it dries and softens, and very short cuts on very fine hair can look sparse.
- **Fringe above the eyebrows** — the usual reason for the appointment in the first place.
- **Clean around the ears and the nape.** This is what makes a cut look intentional.
- **Skip the fade the first time.** Fades need sustained stillness and clipper work close to the skin. Save it for the third or fourth appointment, once they're comfortable in the chair.

## After the First One

Two things make every subsequent haircut easier.

**Go back to the same barber.** Familiarity does most of the work. By the third visit, most children have decided that this is a normal thing that happens, and the whole event stops being remarkable.

**Go regularly.** Long gaps reset the familiarity. Every six to eight weeks keeps it routine and keeps each individual cut short.

Ask for a lock of hair if you want to keep it. Barbers get asked this all the time and are happy to set one aside.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What age should a child get their first haircut?**
There's no medical or developmental answer — it's whenever the hair needs it. Most first cuts happen between nine months and two years.

**Does cutting a baby's hair make it grow thicker?**
No. This is a persistent myth. Hair texture and density are determined at the follicle and aren't changed by cutting.

**Can my child sit on my lap?**
At most barbershops, yes, and it's the standard approach for the youngest children. Just mention it when you arrive.

**What if my child refuses completely?**
Stop, and come back another day. Barbers deal with this regularly and would rather reschedule than create a lasting fear. Many shops won't charge for an attempt that didn't happen.
