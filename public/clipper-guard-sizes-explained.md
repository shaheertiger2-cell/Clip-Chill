# Clipper Guard Sizes Explained: What a #1 Through #8 Actually Looks Like

Clipper numbers are the closest thing men's grooming has to a universal measurement system. They're simple, they're consistent across brands, and knowing them turns a vague haircut request into an exact one.

The catch is that most people know one number — usually whatever they've asked for since high school — and have no idea how it relates to the rest of the scale. Here's the full picture.

## The Numbers Are Just Fractions of an Inch

Every guard number equals that many eighths of an inch of hair left behind. That's the entire system.

| Guard | Length left | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Bald / zero | 0 – 1/16" | Bare or near-bare skin |
| #0.5 | 1/16" | A shadow of colour, scalp clearly visible |
| #1 | 1/8" | Very short stubble, scalp visible |
| #2 | 1/4" | Short and even, scalp faintly visible |
| #3 | 3/8" | The classic short buzz, scalp mostly covered |
| #4 | 1/2" | Short but soft, no scalp showing |
| #5 | 5/8" | Long enough to have a direction |
| #6 | 3/4" | Definitively styleable |
| #7 | 7/8" | Long clipper length, close to scissor territory |
| #8 | 1" | The longest standard guard |

Two practical notes. First, the "zero" is the bare blade with no guard on it, and it still leaves a hair's width behind — a true skin finish usually needs a foil shaver or a razor over the top. Second, some clippers include a lever that shortens the blade slightly within each guard, which is how barbers create the in-between lengths (a "one and a half," a "two and a half") that make smooth fades possible.

## What Each Length Actually Does on a Head

Numbers on a chart are one thing. How they read in a mirror is another.

**Zero to #1** is where contrast lives. At these lengths your scalp shows through and the cut reads as deliberately short. This is the base of any skin fade, and on its own it's an extremely low-maintenance look — though it also shows every scar, mole, and irregularity on your scalp, which some people love and some people don't.

**#2 and #3** are the workhorse buzz cut range. A #3 all over is the single most forgiving self-contained haircut in existence: it suits most head shapes, needs no product, and looks intentional rather than neglected as it grows. A #2 is a step sharper, a step more work.

**#4 to #6** is where a buzz stops being a buzz. At half an inch and up, hair starts to lie in a direction, which means texture and cowlicks begin to matter. If your hair grows in multiple directions at the crown, this is the range where that becomes visible.

**#7 and #8** are rarely used on their own. Their main job is as the top end of a fade — the length the shorter guards blend up into — or for an even, slightly grown-out crop on someone who wants uniformity without going short.

## How Barbers Combine Guards to Build a Fade

A fade is not one guard. It's a sequence of them, blended so no line remains where one ends and the next begins.

A typical mid skin fade might run: bare skin at the very bottom, then a zero, then a #1 through the middle of the sides, then a #2, blending up to a #3 or #4 at the top of the fade before scissor work takes over on top. The barber uses the clipper lever and a technique called clipper-over-comb to erase the boundaries between each step.

This is why "give me a #2 fade" is slightly ambiguous. It usually means the fade's highest point sits around a #2 — but say what you want at the bottom too. "Skin at the bottom, blended up to a 2" is unmistakable.

## Choosing Your Number by Hair Type

Guard numbers behave differently depending on what your hair is doing.

**Coarse or coily hair** appears shorter than the number suggests, because it coils back on itself. A #3 on tightly coiled hair can read like a #1 on straight hair. If you're used to a specific number on straight hair and your texture is different, go one guard longer than you think.

**Fine or thinning hair** shows scalp at longer guards than average. If coverage matters to you, a #4 rather than a #2 will read fuller — though counterintuitively, going very short (#1 or #2 all over) often looks better on genuinely thinning hair than trying to cover it.

**Wavy hair** at #4 and above starts to kick outward rather than lying flat. If you want a clipper cut that stays tidy without product, stopping at a #3 keeps the wave from asserting itself.

**Grey or white hair** shows less contrast against scalp, so short guards look softer than they would in a darker colour.

## Maintenance by Number

Roughly, the shorter the guard, the faster the cut ages:

- **Skin to #1** — noticeably grown out in 1–2 weeks.
- **#2 to #3** — holds its shape for about 3 weeks.
- **#4 to #6** — 4 to 6 weeks before it needs attention.

Hair grows about half an inch a month, so a #2 has doubled in length by week four. That's not a problem if you like the grown-out look, but it explains why some men feel their cut "stops working" after a few weeks while their friend with a #5 seems fine for over a month.

## Cutting It Yourself: What Goes Wrong

Plenty of people own clippers, and a straight all-over buzz at a single guard is genuinely doable at home. Where home cuts fall apart is anywhere two lengths have to meet — the fade — and around the neckline, which you can't see.

If you're maintaining between barber visits, the safe moves are: touch up the same single guard all over, or clean your neckline with a trimmer using a mirror. The unsafe move is attempting to blend two guards on the back of your own head. That's the one that ends with an appointment to fix it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Are guard numbers the same on every clipper brand?**
The numbering standard is consistent — a #2 is 1/4" on Wahl, Andis, or BaBylissPRO. Blade sharpness and motor speed differ, and a very worn blade can leave slightly more length, but the sizes themselves match.

**What number is a standard buzz cut?**
Most people mean a #2 to #4 all over. A #3 is the most common single-guard buzz because it's short enough to be low-maintenance and long enough to look soft.

**Should the top and sides be the same number?**
Only for a true buzz cut. Most cuts use a shorter number on the sides and either a longer guard or scissors on top — that contrast is what gives a haircut shape.
