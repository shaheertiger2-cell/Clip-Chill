# The Best Haircut for a Job Interview (and When to Get It)

Nobody gets hired because of a haircut. But grooming is one of the first signals a person processes about you, before you've said anything, and it's one of the very few variables in an interview you have complete control over.

The bar isn't high. It's not "impressive." It's "clearly deliberate." Here's how to clear it.

## The Timing: Three to Five Days Before

This is the part most people get wrong in both directions.

A haircut the day before looks freshly cut — sharp edges, a hard line-up, and any small unevenness still visible. A haircut three weeks before looks like you didn't think about it.

**Three to five days out** is the window. The cut has softened just enough to look like your hair rather than like a haircut, and it's still holding its shape completely.

It also builds in a buffer. If something is wrong — too short, an uneven line, a neckline you don't like — you have time to have it corrected. The day before, you don't.

## The Cut: Conservative, Clean, Not Extreme

The reliable options across essentially every industry:

**A classic short back and sides.** Tapered or low-faded sides, an inch or two on top, styled to one side or slightly forward. This has read as professional for eighty years and still does.

**A textured crop.** Short, forward-styled, with a low or mid taper. Modern without being loud, and it holds shape through a day without much product.

**A neat side part.** The most conventional option available. Reads as formal, which is either right or slightly stiff depending on the industry.

**A clean buzz cut.** Genuinely professional and completely unfussy — a #2 to #4 all over with a tidy neckline reads as deliberate and requires nothing on the day.

Things that read poorly in a conservative interview: a high skin fade with heavy contrast, hard parts or shaved designs, anything with visible product shine, and any style that requires you to keep touching it.

If you work in a creative field, the bar shifts. A high fade in a design studio is unremarkable. The judgment call is about your industry — but the safe direction is always slightly more conservative than you'd wear on a Saturday.

## The Details That Get Noticed

The haircut is the easy part. These are the things men skip and interviewers register without consciously noting them.

**The neckline.** You never see the back of your own neck, and the person walking you to the meeting room sees nothing else. Get it cleaned up. A tapered neckline is the safer choice, since it stays looking tidy for longer than a blocked one.

**Ears and nose.** Ear hair and nose hair are, unfairly, one of the most noticed grooming details there is. A trimmer takes thirty seconds; face waxing at a barbershop takes ten minutes and does a more thorough job.

**Eyebrows.** Not shaped or thinned — just cleaned up between the brows and any strays trimmed. Threading handles this cleanly and takes about ten minutes.

**The beard, if you have one.** A well-shaped beard is entirely professional in 2026 across nearly every industry. What isn't professional is an unshaped one. If you're keeping the beard, get the cheek line, neckline, and length evened out. A beard trim two to three days before the interview is the right timing — closer than the haircut, because beard hair grows faster.

**Clean-shaven, if that's your look.** Shave the morning of, not the night before. If you have heavy growth, this matters; five o'clock shadow at a 4pm interview is a real possibility.

## Product: Enough to Hold, Not Enough to See

The failure modes are visible product and no product at all.

Matte finishes only — a clay or a matte paste. Shine reads as heavy and dated, and it also makes hair look wet, which nobody is aiming for.

Use less than your usual amount. Interview rooms are warm, days are long, and product that looked right at 8am can look greasy by 2pm.

And style it so you don't need to touch it. Touching your hair during an interview reads as nervousness whether or not you feel nervous. If your style needs a hand run through it every ten minutes, pick a different one for the day.

## The Rest of the Grooming Checklist

Worth running through the night before:

- Nails trimmed and clean — you'll shake hands, and hands are visible on a table the whole time
- Shave, or beard shaped
- Ear, nose, and eyebrow hair addressed
- Teeth
- A light moisturizer for the morning, so skin doesn't look dry under office lighting
- Fragrance used sparingly or not at all — many offices are scent-reduced, and too much is far worse than none

## What About Video Interviews?

The camera changes the emphasis. It shows the top and front of your head, your face, and nothing below the shoulders, and it flattens everything.

Practical adjustments: get the hairline and the top looking right; a fade nobody will see matters less. Overhead lighting exaggerates thinning on top, so front-facing light is more flattering. Skip shine entirely — it reflects badly on camera. And check yourself on the actual camera the day before, since what a phone mirror shows and what a webcam shows are different.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How long before an interview should I get a haircut?**
Three to five days. Long enough that the cut has settled and doesn't look brand new, short enough that it's still holding its shape — and with enough buffer to fix anything you don't like.

**Can I have a beard at a job interview?**
Yes, across nearly every industry, provided it's shaped. A groomed beard reads as intentional. An unmaintained one reads as inattentive.

**Is a fade professional?**
A low or mid taper fade is entirely professional. A high skin fade with strong contrast is bolder and depends more on the industry. When in doubt, go lower.

**Should I dye grey hair for an interview?**
Grey hair is not a liability and colouring it days before an interview introduces a risk of a bad result. A sharp, well-maintained cut does far more for you than colour.

**What if my hair is thinning?**
Go shorter rather than trying to cover it. A short cut with thinning hair reads as deliberate; a long cut over thinning hair draws attention to exactly what you're trying to hide.

**What's the safest cut if I'm unsure?**
A tapered short back and sides with an inch or two on top, styled with a matte product. It has never been wrong in any industry.
